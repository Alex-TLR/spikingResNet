\begin{abstract}
Out-of-distribution (OoD) detection is a critical requirement for deploying deep learning systems in open-world environments, yet most existing methods rely on convolutional networks with high computational and energy demands. Spike-like models, based on standard convolutional backbones in which conventional activations are replaced by leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, are investigated as a computationally efficient alternative for OoD detection. Constrained to a single simulation time step at inference, inherently binary spike activations are produced while the feature-extraction capacity of the underlying backbone is retained, thereby substantially reducing arithmetic complexity relative to conventional CNNs: floating-point MAC operations are reduced by over two orders of magnitude for residual backbones, with the remaining compute dominated by energy-efficient integer accumulations. The interaction between training objectives, feature representation type, population coding, and post-hoc OoD detection methods is systematically studied across multiple backbone architectures and in-distribution benchmarks: CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Both distance-based and scoring-based post-hoc detectors are evaluated. MSE-trained spike-like models with $k$-nearest-neighbor detection match or exceed CNN baselines at a fraction of the arithmetic cost. On the OpenOOD CIFAR-10 benchmark, single-step inference achieves Near-OoD AUROC of 93.91\% and Far-OoD AUROC of 96.63\%; multi-step training with single-step inference further raises these to 97.64\% and 98.86\% (FPR95 of 12.34\% and 5.41\%), outperforming all compared baselines. On CIFAR-100, Near-OoD AUROC of 88.89\% and Far-OoD AUROC of 93.39\% are achieved, exceeding the top CNN entry on the OpenOOD leaderboard. Population coding consistently improves OoD separability and is identified as a prerequisite for multi-step training to yield further gains under single-step inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\PARstart{M}{achine} learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) have become central to modern industrial, scientific, and societal applications. From autonomous vehicles and robotics to medical diagnostics and predictive maintenance, intelligent systems are increasingly deployed in environments where reliability and adaptability are critical. The success of such systems is determined not only by performance on known data but also by their robustness in unexpected or unfamiliar situations.

One of the major challenges in this context is out-of-distribution (OoD) detection, which involves identifying inputs that deviate from the data distribution observed during training. A failure to recognize such unfamiliar inputs can lead to incorrect or hazardous outcomes, especially in safety-critical domains. As a result, OoD detection has emerged as a fundamental component of trustworthy and safe AI, ensuring that models can distinguish between familiar and novel data and respond appropriately~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline, liu2020energy, lee2018simple}.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) remain a dominant choice for OoD detection due to their strong feature extraction capabilities and mature architectures, although recent studies have also investigated transformer-based backbones for this task~\cite{ming2022delving}. A variety of post-hoc scoring methods, including Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP)~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline}, energy-based scores~\cite{liu2020energy}, and Mahalanobis-distance-based detectors~\cite{lee2018simple}, have been proposed to assess predictive confidence and isolate OoD samples without retraining the underlying model. While these approaches establish strong baselines, conventional CNN-based inference relies on dense floating-point activations and a large number of multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations. The resulting significant computational and energy demands limit their applicability in resource-constrained, embedded, or real-time environments.

Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) offer a promising alternative by exploiting a sparse, event-driven computational paradigm. In SNNs, neurons emit discrete spikes only when their membrane potentials exceed a threshold, leading to reduced activity and potentially lower energy consumption during inference. In particular, operating in low-latency regimes, ideally with a single simulation time step $(T=1)$, substantially reduces synaptic operations by substituting floating-point multiply-accumulate operations with sparse integer accumulations. However, this efficiency can trade off against representational capacity: high classification accuracy in SNNs is often achieved via rate-based coding across long temporal integration windows, which require many time steps and diminish potential energy savings. Consequently, a fundamental trade-off arises between accuracy and efficiency in SNN-based systems.

To mitigate these limitations, population coding has emerged as a promising structural complement~\cite{eliasmith2003neural}. By distributing information across groups of neurons rather than single units, population coding can improve robustness, reduce sensitivity to noise, and stabilize training dynamics. Within spiking architectures, population coding can approximate rate-based representations without incurring their temporal latency, making it attractive for low-latency, energy-efficient inference regimes where traditional temporal coding strategies are impractical.

Motivated by these observations, this paper systematically investigates whether single-step spike-like networks can yield discriminative feature representations for post-hoc OoD detection under low-latency, low-compute inference constraints. Specifically, this paper addresses three questions: whether training strategies can be adapted to learn discriminative representations from scratch under single-step inference, whether standard post-hoc OoD detection methods transfer effectively to such representations, and whether population coding further enhances feature separability and robustness. `Spike-like' denotes our single-step ($T=1$) spiking models built on conventional convolutional backbones, in which standard non-linearities are replaced by leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons; `SNN' refers to fully spiking networks with multi-step temporal processing or event-driven execution; `CNN' denotes standard non-spiking convolutional models with full-precision activations. 

Under single-step inference with zero initial membrane state, a spike-like model is mathematically equivalent to a Binarized Neural Network (BNN)~\cite{courbariaux2016binarized}, the LIF leak and reset have no influence on the forward pass, producing hard-quantized outputs natively and bypassing the overhead of conventional quantization schemes. Prior work~\cite{Olber2022DetectionOO} has demonstrated that binary activation patterns extracted post-hoc from trained CNNs are informative for OoD detection. The present approach differs in two key respects: representations are learned from scratch using full multi-step LIF dynamics and surrogate-gradient training, exploiting a training/inference asymmetry that has no analogue in static BNNs, and population coding is incorporated as an SNN-specific output mechanism absent from standard BNN pipelines (see Section~\ref{sec:network}).

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Low-Latency Spike-Like OoD Framework:} A spike-like OoD detection framework is introduced, based on standard convolutional backbones with single-step LIF activations that eliminate full-precision floating-point activations and reduce MAC operations by one to two orders of magnitude relative to CNN baselines, while retaining compatibility with standard post-hoc detection pipelines. State-of-the-art (SOTA)-competitive OoD detection is demonstrated from a BNN-equivalent inference baseline ($T=1$), with performance progressively improved through multi-step training and population coding.
  \item \textit{Systematic Evaluation of Training Objectives:} A comparative analysis of training objectives (mean squared error (MSE), cross-entropy (CE), and spike-count loss (CL)) and multi-step training configurations is conducted, showing that MSE-trained models yield the most discriminative representations and that multi-step training is beneficial only when combined with sufficient population-coding expansion.
  \item \textit{Extensive Post-Hoc Benchmarking:} Distance-based ($k$-nearest-neighbor (kNN)) and six scoring-based post-hoc OoD detectors are evaluated on spike-like feature representations, demonstrating SOTA-competitive performance on the OpenOOD CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 benchmarks at substantially reduced arithmetic cost.
  \item \textit{Isolation of Population Coding as a Performance Enabler:} Population coding is identified as the single most consistent performance enabler, independently improving both distance-based and scoring-based detection, and established as a prerequisite for multi-step training gains under single-step inference.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background (Research Context)}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Out-of-Distribution Detection}

The OoD detection task is to determine whether a test sample $x \in \mathcal{X}$ originates from the training distribution $P_{\text{train}}(x)$. When a sample is out-of-distribution (i.e. $x \nsim P_{\text{train}}(x)$), feature representations and confidence scores may be misleading, typically such inputs are flagged to prevent overconfident or erroneous decisions. Let $P_{\text{train}}(x, y)$ denote the joint distribution of inputs and labels observed during training, and let $P_{\text{train}}(x)$ denote its marginal. OoD detection can then be formulated as a binary hypothesis test:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:01a}
\mathcal{H}_0: \; x \sim P_{\text{train}}(x),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:01b}
\mathcal{H}_1: \; x \sim P_{\text{out}}(x), \quad P_{\text{out}} \neq P_{\text{train}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_0$ corresponds to in-distribution samples and $\mathcal{H}_1$ to out-of-distribution samples. Reliable OoD detection is essential for deploying deep learning models in open-world environments where unexpected or anomalous samples may occur.

\subsection{Convolutional and Spiking Neural Network Backbones}

Conventional CNN backbones forward-propagate continuous, full-precision activations across all layers. For a standard convolutional or fully connected layer, the output activation vector $\mathbf{a}$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cnn}
\mathbf{a} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}$ is the weight matrix, $\mathbf{b}$ the bias, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ a continuous non-linear activation such as ReLU. Because all activation tensors are evaluated exhaustively at inference time regardless of input, the computational load is high and independent of input activity.

SNNs replace the continuous activation with a leaky LIF neuron. Over discrete simulation time steps $t = 1, \ldots, T$, the membrane potential $u_i^t$ of neuron $i$ integrates weighted pre-synaptic spikes while decaying:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lif}
u_i^t = \beta\, u_i^{t-1} + \sum_{j} w_{ij}\, s_j^t,
\end{equation}
where $\beta \in (0,1)$ is the temporal decay factor and $s_j^t \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary spike from pre-synaptic neuron $j$. When $u_i^t$ exceeds a firing threshold $\vartheta$, an output spike is emitted and the potential is reset:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:spike}
s_i^t = H\!\left(u_i^t - \vartheta\right), \qquad u_i^t \leftarrow u_i^t(1 - s_i^t) + u_{\text{reset}}\,s_i^t,
\end{equation}
where $H(\cdot)$ is the Heaviside step function. Because computation is triggered only by spikes, energy consumption scales with spike activity and sparsity~\cite{2021-eshraghian}, unlike CNNs where cost is fixed.

\subsection{Single-Step Inference and BNN Equivalence}

When inference is restricted to a single simulation time step ($T=1$) starting from zero initial membrane state ($u_i^0 = 0$), the decay term $\beta\, u_i^{t-1}$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:lif}) vanishes. The LIF dynamics collapse to a spatial threshold operation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:t1}
u_i = \sum_{j} w_{ij}\, s_j, \qquad s_i = H\!\left(u_i - \vartheta\right).
\end{equation}
This is mathematically equivalent to the binary threshold activation of a BNNs~\cite{courbariaux2016binarized}: the model fires if and only if the weighted input sum exceeds $\vartheta$. Deeper layers thus process inherently binary arrays, replacing floating-point MACs with sparse integer accumulations.

Because the Heaviside function has zero derivative almost everywhere, standard backpropagation is not applicable. Surrogate-gradient training replaces the true derivative with a smooth proxy $\sigma'(u)$ during the backward pass, enabling gradient-based optimization from scratch. Feature representations are extracted post-hoc either as binary spike patterns $\mathbf{s}$ or as continuous membrane potentials $\mathbf{u}$ from intermediate layers. Input images are used directly as grayscale or RGB inputs; the first convolutional layer together with the spike activation implicitly converts pixel intensities into spike activations, avoiding the overhead of rate- or time-based input encoding.

\subsection{Population Coding}

To counteract the potential loss of representational capacity caused by constraining inference to $T=1$, population coding~\cite{eliasmith2003neural} is applied at the output layer. Instead of assigning each of the $C$ target classes to a single output neuron, the final linear layer is expanded by a factor $E$, yielding $C \times E$ output units. Class predictions are derived by aggregating over each sub-population:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:popcoding}
\tilde{z}_c = \mathcal{A}\!\left(\left\{ z_{c,e} \right\}_{e=1}^{E}\right),
\end{equation}
where $z_{c,e}$ is the activation (spike count or membrane potential) of the $e$-th neuron assigned to class $c$, and $\mathcal{A}$ is an aggregation operator (e.g., mean or sum). By distributing information across groups of neurons, population coding improves robustness to noise, stabilizes training dynamics, and reshapes the learned feature space towards greater intra-class compactness and inter-class separation, enhancing both classification accuracy and OoD discriminability without increasing the computational footprint of the convolutional backbone.

In many real-world applications, such as autonomous driving or industrial monitoring, systems must detect inputs that deviate from the training distribution in order to ensure safe and reliable operation. This requirement has motivated extensive research on OoD detection in deep learning.

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{CNN-based OoD detection.}
Existing CNN-based OoD detection frameworks are predominantly applied post-hoc to feature representations of pre-trained classifiers. These inference-time strategies fall into two primary paradigms. Scoring-based methods derive a scalar anomaly metric from individual feature vectors or final logit distributions, including Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP)~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline}, energy-based scores~\cite{liu2020energy}, Virtual Logit Matching (VIM)~\cite{wang2022vim}, and Activation Shaping (ASH)~\cite{djurisic2022ash}. More recent approaches such as GEN~\cite{10203747} and Fast Decision Boundary Detection (FDBD)~\cite{liu2024fast} analyze decision geometry and boundary margins to enhance the separability between in-distribution and OoD samples. Distance-based methods evaluate spatial displacement relative to in-distribution references in the learned latent space, including Mahalanobis-distance detectors~\cite{lee2018simple}, Nearest Class Mean (NCM)~\cite{huo2025ncm}, and $k$-nearest-neighbor (kNN) algorithms~\cite{sun2022knn}.

Beyond these post-hoc approaches, several methods explicitly model the structure of feature representations. Examples include feature-space density estimation and density-ratio models~\cite{sun2022knn, zhang2022ood}, clustering-based repre\-sen\-tation learning approaches~\cite{sinhamahapatra2022all}, and generative approaches based on latent diffusion models~\cite{10.1145/3731715.3733326}. Recent work has also explored the geometry of classifier parameters themselves; WeiPer~\cite{granz2024weiper} estimates OoD uncertainty through perturbations of class projection weights and demonstrates that classifier-weight information can provide complementary anomaly-detection signals. These methods aim to improve the separation between in-distribution and OoD samples directly in the learned embedding space.

Another important research direction focuses on improving OoD detection during training. Hendrycks \textit{et al.}~\cite{NEURIPS2019} demonstrated that incorporating self-supervised learning objectives produces more robust feature representations and improves uncertainty estimation, leading to enhanced OoD detection performance. Outlier Exposure (OE)~\cite{hendrycks2018deep} further improves OoD awareness by introducing auxiliary outlier datasets during training and encouraging low-confidence predictions on anomalous samples. A data-augmentation strategy was proposed in PixMix~\cite{Hendrycks_2022_CVPR} that mixes training images with structurally complex patterns such as fractals, yielding simultaneous improvements in corruption robustness, calibration, anomaly detection, and OoD performance. Similarly, Logit Normalization (LogitNorm)~\cite{pmlr-v162-wei22d} modifies the cross-entropy objective by constraining the norm of the logit vector, reducing network overconfidence and improving the separability between in-distribution and OoD samples. Mixture Outlier Exposure (MixOE)~\cite{Zhang_2023_WACV} extends exposure-based training by constructing mixed inlier-outlier samples, improving OoD detection in fine-grained classification tasks.
Virtual Outlier Synthesis (VOS)~\cite{du2022towards} generates synthetic outliers in low-density regions of the feature space to facilitate unknown-aware learning without requiring large auxiliary OoD datasets.

In parallel, improved evaluation methodologies and benchmark suites have refined the assessment of OoD detectors and highlighted limitations of earlier evaluation protocols~\cite{kuan2022back, yang2022openood}. While most of the above approaches were originally developed for convolutional neural networks, many can be adapted to spiking neural networks by operating on either spike representations or membrane potentials. However, the effectiveness of transferring OoD detection techniques to sparse and event-driven representations remains an active area of research.


\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{OoD detection with spiking networks.}
OoD detection in spiking neural networks has only recently been explored. In~\cite{2023-martinez}, spike-count activations across layers were used to derive explainable OoD indicators, achieving competitive performance on both static and event-based datasets. In~\cite{terresescudero2025}, the robustness of fully spiking networks in open-world scenarios was investigated, and improved generalization under forward-only training schemes was demonstrated.

Uncertainty estimation in SNNs has also been studied as a related problem. Spike-based confidence measures~\cite{Sun_2023} and Bayesian formulations~\cite{prabodh2024} indicate that spike activity statistics can provide meaningful uncertainty estimates that can be leveraged for OoD detection.

%While these existing approaches demonstrate competitive performance, they rely on multi-step temporal processing or event-driven execution to accumulate sufficient spike statistics. In contrast, the framework proposed in this work operates strictly within a single-step ($T=1$) spike-like regime, eliminating temporal execution latency while preserving computational efficiency through native activation sparsity.

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Training and architectural advances in SNNs.}
Recent progress in surrogate-gradient learning has enabled effective training of deep spiking networks using gradient-based optimization~\cite{neftci2019surrogate, zenke2021remarkable, eshraghian2023training}. Architectures such as Spiking-ResNet~\cite{2022-fang, 2021-zheng} and directly trained deep SNNs~\cite{2020-rathi} have achieved performance comparable to CNNs on standard benchmarks. Additional improvements include information-theoretic loss functions~\cite{2022-Guo} and learnable membrane dynamics~\cite{2025-shen}, which enhance representation quality under limited temporal depth. These advances are particularly relevant for low-latency deployments, as they preserve the discriminative structure of learned feature representations even as the temporal integration window approaches its minimum.

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Population coding in spiking networks.}  
Population coding distributes information across groups of neurons, leading to robust and noise-tolerant representations~\cite{Tkacik2010Optimal, Tripp2012Population}. Its application in machine learning includes temporal classification~\cite{Pan2019NeuralPopulation} and reinforcement learning~\cite{Zhang2021PopulationCoding}. However, its role in OoD detection has not been extensively studied, and its potential as a mechanism for improving feature separability in both spiking and dense representations remains largely unexplored.

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Binarized and quantized neural networks.}
Binarized Neural Networks (BNNs)~\cite{courbariaux2016binarized} constrain both weights and activations to binary values ($\{-1,+1\}$ or $\{0,1\}$), replacing multiply-accumulate operations with XNOR-popcount operations and reducing memory and energy consumption substantially. XNOR-Net~\cite{rastegari2016xnor} demonstrated that this idea scales to deep convolutional architectures on ImageNet-scale tasks. More broadly, the field of quantized neural networks (QNNs) has shown that low-bit-width inference can approach floating-point accuracy across a wide range of vision tasks, and that the arithmetic savings are hardware-realizable on both conventional and neuromorphic accelerators.

%The relationship between single-step spiking networks and BNNs is an important one and is explicitly acknowledged in this work. When a LIF neuron is simulated for a single time step starting from zero membrane potential, the leak factor $\beta$ and the reset mechanism have no mathematical influence on the output: the neuron fires if and only if the weighted input sum exceeds the threshold, which is precisely the behavior of a deterministic binary threshold (sign) activation. At $T=1$ with zero initial state, the spike-like model is therefore equivalent to a BNN or threshold-activation network. This work starts from this BNN-like baseline deliberately and exploits two complementary mechanisms to improve OoD discriminability beyond what a statically trained BNN achieves: (i)~a training-inference asymmetry in which the number of simulation steps used during training is progressively increased beyond one, allowing genuine LIF temporal dynamics and multi-step surrogate-gradient learning to shape the learned feature representations while inference remains fixed at $T=1$; and (ii)~population coding at the output layer, which is an SNN-inspired representation strategy with no direct counterpart in standard BNN pipelines. Neither the training-inference asymmetry nor the role of population coding in OoD detection have been studied in the BNN or QNN literature, which motivates the SNN framing adopted here.

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{Computational and energy considerations.}  
The compu\-tational complexity of CNNs is typically measured using floating-point operations (FLOPs) or multiply-accumulate (MAC) counts. In contrast, SNNs rely on spike-triggered synaptic operations (SynOps), which depend on activity and sparsity~\cite{roy2019towards, davies2018loihi, plagwitz2023to}. For spike-like models, deeper layers primarily involve accumulate-only (AC) operations, reducing arithmetic complexity~\cite{chen2023training}. Comparisons between MACs and SynOps are commonly used as a hardware-agnostic proxy for computational efficiency~\cite{lemaire2023estimation}. The utilized network architectures are based on convolutional backbones augmented with key properties of spiking neural networks. Standard ReLU activations used in CNNs are replaced by LIF neurons, which produce binary spike outputs while preserving spatial feature-extraction capabilities. Both sparse feature vectors and dense feature representations can be extracted and subsequently used for post-hoc OoD detection. These representations can be obtained either as sparse spike patterns (1 indicating a spike and 0 indicating silence) or as dense feature vectors derived from membrane potentials in the penultimate or output layer.

The number of simulation time steps $T$ employed during training and inference can be selected flexibly based on deployment constraints. In prior SNN work on OoD detection, inference commonly required tens of time steps to accumulate stable firing rates~\cite{2023-martinez}; classification-focused models occasionally operated with fewer~\cite{2021deep}.

In the limiting case of $T=1$, the network operates as a binarized spike-like model. Temporal integration is suppressed, and neuron behavior reduces to a purely spatial threshold operation: a binary activation is emitted if the immediate pre-synaptic input drives the membrane potential past the firing threshold $\vartheta$ (see Section~\ref{sec:background}, Eq.~(\ref{eq:t1})). This preserves activation sparsity and computational efficiency while producing discriminative feature representations suitable for post-hoc OoD detection.

To further exploit the performance-efficiency trade-off, models can be trained with multiple time steps to engage full LIF temporal dynamics, then evaluated under single-step inference ($T=1$) during feature extraction. This training-inference asymmetry allows the feature manifold to be shaped by richer temporal learning while keeping deployment cost minimal.

In this work, the given backbone architectures are combined with spiking activation layers. Depending on the underlying backbone, the resulting models are referred to as either Spike-ConvNet or Spike-ResNet architectures. In all cases, LIF activations are employed with parameters $\beta=0.95$, threshold $\vartheta=0.25$, and reset $=0$. Residual blocks in Spike-ResNet architectures consist of multiple convolutional layers followed by BatchNorm2d and LIF activations. Skip connections are applied to spiking feature maps using element-wise addition, after which the outputs are clamped to a maximum value of 1. Downsampling is performed using convolutional layers with a stride of 2, followed by LIF activation, thereby preserving spiking characteristics across network stages. The key architectural characteristics of all implemented backbones are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:network-backbones}.

Population coding is implemented by expanding the final linear layer to size $C\times E$ (where $C$ is the number of output classes and $E$ is the expansion factor): neurons from the penultimate layer are fully connected to all $C\times E$ neurons in the final layer. The per-class response is then computed by aggregating $E$ outputs for each class, which increases only the number of final-layer parameters while leaving the convolutional backbone unchanged.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Training Procedure}
All backbones were trained under identical experimental conditions. The loss functions, preprocessing, augmentation, and optimization details are summarized below. When population coding is used, final-layer outputs are partitioned into class-wise sub-populations before the loss is applied: summed spike counts are used for spike-count objectives (MSE, CL), while mean membrane potentials are used for membrane-based objectives (CE).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SNN + MSE (spike-pattern-based mean-square error):} Optimization is performed on the final-layer spike pattern. Spike counts are accumulated over the simulation window, and the mean-squared error with respect to the target representation is minimized. No per-channel normalization of input image is applied for this objective, and data augmentation is restricted to geometric transformations (random horizontal flip and random crop).

\item \textbf{SNN + CL (spike count-loss):} Optimization is performed directly on integer spike-count targets. As with MSE-based training, no per-channel normalization is applied, and augmentation is restricted to geometric transformations.

\item \textbf{SNN + CE (membrane-potential-based cross-entropy):} Optimization is performed on the continuous membrane potentials of the final layer. Considering that the target is not spike count, the standard per-channel mean-standard deviation normalization is applied, together with both geometric and photometric augmentations.

\item \textbf{CNN + CE:} Identical backbones are trained as regular convolutional neural networks using cross-entropy loss on output logits to provide a conventional CNN baseline for comparison. Standard normalization and both geometric and photometric augmentations are applied.
\end{itemize}

When population coding is used, final-layer outputs are reshaped into class-wise populations and aggregated before the loss is applied (see Subsection~\ref{sec:population-coding}). Optimization was carried out using the Adam optimizer with standard parameters and a weight decay of $10^{-4}$. A cyclical learning rate schedule (OneCycleLR) with an initial learning rate of $2\cdot10^{-4}$ was used to improve convergence over 400 training epochs. All models were trained with a batch size of 64.

\subsection{Post-Hoc OoD Detection Methods}
All detectors are applied post-hoc to pre-trained feature representations without additional training. Feature vectors are extracted from either the penultimate layer (membrane potentials or spike patterns) or the final layer (logits), depending on the method. For scoring-based methods, only dense membrane-potential features are used to ensure a fair comparison with CNN baselines. All thresholds are set at the score value corresponding to 95\% true-positive rate on the joint ID/OoD validation set, following the standard FPR@TPR95 protocol.

Six scoring-based methods operate on final-layer logits: \textbf{MSP}~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline} and \textbf{MLS} require no hyperparameters; \textbf{Energy (ENGY)}~\cite{liu2020energy} uses temperature $T=1.0$; \textbf{ODIN}~\cite{liang2018enhancing} uses $T=1000$ with input perturbation magnitude $\varepsilon=0.0014$; \textbf{VIM}~\cite{wang2022vim} uses residual-energy weighting coefficient $\alpha=0.1$; \textbf{ASH}~\cite{djurisic2022ash} retains the top $p=25\%$ of activations and zeroes the rest. The single distance-based method used is \textbf{kNN}~\cite{sun2022knn}, which operates on penultimate-layer features and uses $k=10$ nearest neighbours.

Fig.~\ref{fig:feature-extraction-pipeline-narrow} illustrates the experimental pipeline. When population coding is used, final-layer outputs are reshaped and aggregated per class before scoring.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Performance of feature extraction and OoD detection is quantified using:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AUROC:} Measures the probability that a randomly chosen ID sample receives a higher confidence score than a randomly chosen OoD sample. Higher AUROC indicates better discriminative capability.
    \item \textbf{FPR95:} Represents the fraction of OoD samples incorrectly classified as ID when the true positive rate for ID samples is 95\%. Lower FPR95 indicates more reliable OoD detection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Inference-Time Computational Analysis}
As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:related}, CNN complexity is commonly reported using floating-point operation (FLOP) counts, typically expressed as the number of multiply--accumulate (MAC) operations that dominate convolutional inference. Hardware-agnostic estimates of these costs are provided by the architecture-level tool \texttt{ptflops}~\cite{ptflops}. By contrast, spiking networks perform event-driven computation: synaptic operations occur only when spikes are emitted, so cost is more naturally expressed in synaptic operations (SynOps) or spike-triggered accumulations. In the spike-like models studied here, activations are binary and temporal integration is suppressed, which reduces arithmetic complexity.

To enable a practical comparison, inference-time operation counts are computed for each spike-like network and its CNN counterpart while keeping the backbone architecture identical. In a CNN, a MAC operation is required for each convolutional output element. For spike-like networks processing static RGB inputs, MACs are required at the input/first layer (to process real-valued pixel intensities); in deeper layers, binary activations allow multiplications to be replaced by accumulate-only (AC) operations. Floating-point operations are still involved in normalization and some auxiliary layers, while the dominant inference work in spike-like models is shifted toward integer additions. Although the energy per operation is dependent on CMOS technology and implementation details~\cite{lemaire2023estimation}, a hardware-agnostic proxy for relative compute cost is provided by aggregating MAC and AC counts.

MAC and AC counts for CNN and spike-like models on RGB inputs of size $32\times32$ are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:computation-comparison}, following the counting conventions of~\cite{ptflops, lemaire2023estimation}.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:computation-comparison}, the MAC counts of spike-like networks are reduced by approximately $184\times$ (ResNet-18), $101\times$ (ResNet-10), and $2.5\times$ (ConvNet) relative to their CNN counterparts at the base expansion ($E=1$). The comparatively modest reduction for ConvNet reflects the dominance of the first real-valued layer, whose MACs are not eliminated by binary activations. The number of ACs grows moderately with the population-coding expansion rate $E$: for ResNet-18, the AC count increases from 172.48~M at $E=1$ to 183.76~M at $E=50$, a relative increase of approximately 6.5\%. A similar trend is observed for ResNet-10 (69.80~M to 84.03~M, $\approx$20\%) and ConvNet (0.82~M to 1.14~M, $\approx$39\%). The MAC component, by contrast, remains constant across all expansion values, as population coding affects only the final output layer. These results confirm that the computational advantage of spike-like inference is largely preserved even at larger expansion factors, with only a modest increase in AC cost.

This experiment evaluates scoring-based OoD detection methods applied to membrane-potential features extracted from spike-like residual networks trained with the MSE objective and operating at a single simulation time step. The evaluated methods include Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP), Energy, Maximum Logit Score (MLS), ASH, and Virtual Logit Matching (VIM). In contrast to the previous experiment, where distance-based kNN detection was performed on penultimate-layer features, here post-hoc anomaly scores are computed from final-layer representations (with the exception of VIM, which uses both penultimate and final layer features). Population-coding expansions are varied to assess whether distributed output representations influence the separability of final-layer features and the effectiveness of scoring-based OoD detection.

For the spike-ConvNet backbone, the resulting OoD scores are uninformative regardless of the training objective, and its results are therefore omitted. Furthermore, substantially lower AUROC is yielded by spike-pattern features compared to membrane-potential features across all evaluated methods, so only membrane-potential feature results are reported. For the CNN backbone, population-coded scoring results are not reported because they were consistently worse than the corresponding non-population baseline.

Results for the spike-ResNet10 and spike-ResNet18 backbones are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cifar10-experiment-2}. Several consistent observations are evident. First, AUROC values comparable to, and in some settings higher than, those of the CNN baseline for scoring-based post-hoc detection are achieved by the proposed spike-like models. Second, AUROC is generally improved by increasing the population-coding expansion, indicating that distributed output representations are beneficial for final-layer scoring. Third, the strongest method overall in this setup is VIM, which leverages both penultimate- and final-layer information and reaches the highest AUROC values across the reported configurations. Finally, although AUROC trends are strong, many FPR95 values remain relatively high, indicating room for further improvement in operating-point performance.

These results demonstrate that effective application of standard post-hoc OoD scoring methods is enabled by single-step spike-like models trained with the MSE objective, particularly when dense membrane-potential features are combined with population coding.

This experiment extends the first CIFAR-10 experiment: the spike-ResNet18 backbone is trained with the MSE objective across four training-step budgets $T \in \{1, 2, 4, 8\}$, while inference is kept at a single time step in all cases. The effect of training depth is evaluated with both kNN (on penultimate-layer membrane-potential features) and VIM, and both sets of results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cifar10-experiment-3}.

Two distinct regimes emerge from the color patterns in the tables. Without population coding ($E=1$), increasing the number of training steps does not consistently improve OoD performance and, in several cases, substantially degrades it. This indicates that temporal integration during training restructures the internal representations in a way that is poorly suited to single-step feature extraction when the output layer is unexpanded. With sufficient population coding ($E \geq 25$), the picture changes markedly: multi-step training then yields clearly stronger OoD separation than single-step training. The benefit of multi-step training, therefore, depends on the presence of an adequately expanded output layer to relay the richer gradient signal back to the penultimate layer.

Population coding plays a dual role here. In addition to amplifying the effect of multi-step training, it independently improves OoD performance even at $T=1$, as already observed in the preceding experiments. The two mechanisms are therefore complementary: population coding stabilizes and enhances the penultimate-layer feature structure, while multi-step training further refines it when population coding provides a sufficient training signal.

A further observation concerns the relative merit of detection strategies. The kNN detector applied to penultimate-layer membrane-potential features achieves performance comparable to, and in several configurations exceeding, the best scoring-based method (VIM) evaluated on penultimate- and final-layer features. This is notable because kNN operates directly on raw penultimate features without any calibration or transformation, suggesting that well-structured penultimate representations alone can be sufficient for competitive OoD detection.

Overall, the interaction between population coding, training depth, and detector type is more complex than on CIFAR-10, and no single configuration dominates all metrics.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper presented a spike-like OoD detection framework built on standard convolutional backbones with leaky integrate-and-fire activations constrained to single-step ($T=1$) inference. The approach reduces floating-point MAC operations by one to two orders of magnitude relative to CNN baselines, with remaining compute expressed as energy-efficient integer accumulations, while retaining full compatibility with standard post-hoc detection pipelines. Four contributions emerge from the systematic experimental evaluation on the OpenOOD CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 benchmarks.

\textit{First}, it was demonstrated that MSE-trained spike-like models with single-step inference yield well-structured penultimate-layer membrane-potential representations suitable for distance-based OoD detection. With kNN applied to these features, AUROC values matching or exceeding CNN baselines are achieved under SOTA-competitive conditions at substantially reduced arithmetic cost, answering the core question of whether single-step spike-like networks can provide discriminative representations for post-hoc detection.

\textit{Second}, standard post-hoc scoring methods — MSP, Energy, ODIN, MLS, ASH, and VIM — transfer effectively to spike-like final-layer membrane-potential features. Population coding consistently improves AUROC across all scoring methods as the expansion increases. VIM achieves the highest AUROC overall, while the simpler kNN detector on raw penultimate features remains competitive without any post-hoc calibration, demonstrating that well-structured spike-like representations alone are sufficient for competitive OoD detection.

\textit{Third}, multi-step training with single-step inference is shown to further improve OoD separability, but only when population coding provides a sufficiently expanded output layer. Without adequate expansion, multi-step training disrupts rather than improves the penultimate-layer feature structure. With sufficient expansion, training depth and population coding interact constructively, jointly shaping more discriminative representations. This training–inference asymmetry enables higher performance without sacrificing inference efficiency.

\textit{Fourth}, population coding is identified as the single most consistent performance enabler across all experimental settings and both benchmarks. It independently improves distance-based and scoring-based detection, and is established as a prerequisite for multi-step training gains under single-step inference. On CIFAR-10, the best single-step configuration reaches Near-OoD AUROC of 93.91\% and Far-OoD AUROC of 96.63\%; multi-step training with single-step inference ($T{=}2$, $E{=}50$) further improves this to 97.64\% and 98.86\%, outperforming all listed SOTA baselines on both metrics. On the more challenging CIFAR-100 benchmark, Near-OoD AUROC of 88.89\% and Far-OoD AUROC of 93.39\% are achieved, exceeding the best CNN result reported on the OpenOOD leaderboard, at substantially reduced computational cost.
